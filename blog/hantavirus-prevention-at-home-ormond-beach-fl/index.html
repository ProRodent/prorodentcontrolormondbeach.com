Hantavirus and Your Ormond Beach Home: What Cuts the Risk

Reading about hantavirus can be alarming, so start with proportion. The CDC lists it among diseases that rodents can spread, and it treats rodent control and careful cleanup as the main protection. That means the useful actions are practical ones that any household can take.

What the CDC emphasizes.

The CDC says people are usually exposed by breathing in minute particles that may hold the virus and originate in rodent urine, droppings or saliva. That explains why its advice departs from normal housecleaning. Risk comes from disturbing dust, and merely spotting a mouse is not the danger.

  • Close every hole and gap that lets rodents in.
  • Catch the ones that are already indoors.
  • Clean waste while it is damp and never by dry sweeping.
  • Keep food in sealed containers and clear away clutter that offers shelter.
  • Put on gloves for the job and wash your hands when finished.

Those five lines sum up the prevention plan, and the rest of this guide expands them for an Ormond Beach home.

Where exposure tends to happen.

Closed, rarely visited spaces concentrate risk. A sealed garage, a seasonal home that has been shut for months, a shed, a storage unit and an attic fit that description. Opening such a space after a long time and cleaning at once is the situation the CDC warns about.

Opening a closed space safely.

Before you go into a long-closed room, open doors and windows and let it air out for a while. Keep children and pets outside. Do not start by sweeping or vacuuming. Look at what is there, then decide whether the job is something you want to do yourself.

The prevention side: keep them out.

An animal that never gets in cannot leave waste. That is why sealing is the heart of our approach. Metal flashing, heavy mesh and hard-cure sealant close the gaps around pipes, vents and door edges. Foam and plastic do not hold up to teeth. Our exclusion service covers the work.

Why we avoid dry cleanup.

A technician using dry methods in a contaminated attic would raise the very dust the CDC cautions about. Our attic cleanup page describes the wet approach we follow. If your attic has a large amount of waste, it is worth leaving to someone equipped for it.

A word on symptoms.

We are a pest control company, not a medical provider, and we cannot diagnose anything. If you were exposed to rodent waste and then feel unwell, especially with fever or trouble breathing, see a physician promptly and mention the exposure.

Small habits that add up.

Food storage is the easiest change. Pour dry goods and pet food into containers that close tightly, and keep the lids on. Pick up fallen fruit, bag trash firmly and rinse the bins. Without a meal, a house loses much of its appeal.

Next, reduce shelter. Move firewood and boards onto racks that sit clear of walls, trim shrubs back from the siding and keep cartons off the floor in the garage. Each change takes away a hiding place, and together they make the building less welcoming.

Finally, keep an eye out. A monthly look with a flashlight at baseboards, under sinks and around the garage door catches signs while they are small.

Frequently raised points.

Is hantavirus common?

According to the CDC the disease is uncommon. Even so, the agency spells out particular precautions around rodent waste, because the outcome can be severe.

Through what route does exposure happen?

Mostly by inhaling dust that carries virus from dried urine, droppings or saliva. A broom or vacuum can throw that dust into the air.

Do all rodents carry hantavirus?

No. Only certain species do, and the species vary by region. We cannot tell by sight whether a dropping carries it, so we treat all waste with care.

Would my household vacuum be acceptable?

No. The CDC recommends against both sweeping and vacuuming, and points to damp cleaning instead.

Who should I talk to about symptoms?

A physician. If you were around rodent waste and feel unwell, tell the doctor about that exposure.
