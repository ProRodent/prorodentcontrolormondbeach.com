A Checklist for Part-Year Homes in Ormond Beach

A house left empty for months offers rodents both time and quiet. Use the list below before you lock up, again when you walk in, and as a way to open the place without raising dust.

Plenty of homes in Ormond Beach are lived in only part of the year. We have no figures on how many, and we will not offer a number. What matters is the pattern: a closed house, little movement, and long gaps between visits are comfortable conditions for a rodent.

Before you leave.

Think of departure day as a handoff. You want the house to offer nothing and to be hard to enter. Start with the kitchen and finish at the exterior.

Kitchen and pantry.Empty perishables, seal dry goods in tubs.Removes the main attraction.

A walk outside before the door locks.

Look at every side of the building. Check the vent covers, the garage door seal and any pipe or cable entering a wall. Photograph each side. The pictures serve as your baseline when you return.

While you are away.

If possible, arrange for someone to walk through every few weeks. They should check the pantry, the garage and the areas under sinks. Ask them to report pellets, odor or chewed items by text, and to send pictures. Early notice makes the repair smaller.

Arrival day: open, then look.

Leave the broom alone at first. Let fresh air move through the open doors and windows, and keep children and pets outdoors meanwhile. After that, tour each room slowly with a flashlight.

  • Look for pellets along baseboards, in drawers and on shelves.
  • Check cabinets under sinks for chewed pipe wrap.
  • Peek in the garage and shed for nests or shredded material.
  • Sniff for musty or sharp odors.
  • Note any scratching sounds when the house is quiet.

If you find evidence.

Hold off on cleaning until you know what you have. Wet cleaning is the method the CDC describes: gloves, disinfectant on the waste, paper towels, a bag. The safety box below links the full steps. Never sweep or vacuum droppings.

If the signs are heavy or you find nests, call before you start. We can inspect, trap and seal, and cleanup is quoted separately if you want it. See our inspection page for how a first visit runs.

Make it a yearly routine.

Keep the checklist where you keep your keys. Repeat the same steps each season, and update it with anything you learn. Over time, you will see whether signs are rare or recurring, and that tells you whether sealing work is worth doing.

Who should hold the key.

Pick one person to be your contact for the empty house, and give them this checklist in writing. A property manager, a cleaner or a neighbor you trust can each do the job, as long as they know what to look at and how to reach you.

Ask them to avoid cleaning anything they find. A report with pictures is more useful than a tidy floor, because the pellets and nest material show a technician where the animals traveled. Tell them to open the windows and step out if they notice a strong odor, and to phone before doing more.

When your return date is set, give it to the dispatcher. A walk-through a week before you arrive means the house can be ready when you are, with traps set and gaps closed.

Before you call: a careful way to treat pellets and nests.

Use this list each time pellets, urine stains or torn nesting turn up inside.

  • Aged pellets disintegrate when brushed, which is why a light mist of disinfectant comes before any towel or hand.
  • Stale air concentrates whatever floats in it, so let the space breathe for about half an hour before you start.
  • Count every stained surface as contaminated: gloves during, a handwash after, with no shortcuts.
  • Disinfectant needs contact time, so use plenty and let it sit on the waste before wiping.
  • Keep carcasses off bare hands. Bag them, then bin the bag.
  • Fever that will not break, or breathlessness, after contact with rodent waste is a reason to see a physician.

Short answers before you pick up the phone.

What matters most before leaving?

Food, trash and openings. Remove or seal anything edible, empty the bins and walk the outside to close obvious gaps.

Should someone check the house while I am away?

A periodic visit by a neighbor, a property manager or a cleaner is useful. Give them a short list of what to look for.

What should I do on arrival?

Throw the doors and windows open and give the house a good airing before touching anything. Observe first and clean afterward.

Do I need to wear protection?

Gloves are wise, and the CDC discusses respirators for heavy waste. Wet cleaning is the main safeguard.

Can you inspect before I arrive?

Often yes. Call with your date and the dispatcher will say what is open.
