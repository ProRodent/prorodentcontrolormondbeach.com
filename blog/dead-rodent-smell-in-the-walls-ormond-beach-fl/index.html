The smell is unmistakable to anyone who has met it. It is heavy and sweet with a sharp edge, and it can seem to move from room to room. Humid Florida air carries it farther and makes it feel stronger on muggy days, so a faint whiff in the morning can become obvious by the afternoon.

How to narrow down the source.

Odor rarely points straight at its source. Air moves through walls, ducts and attic spaces, so the strongest spot may sit a few feet from the actual carcass. Still, a methodical sniff test narrows the search.

  • Go room to room and mark where the smell peaks.
  • Check near floor vents, return grilles and ceiling registers.
  • Note whether the smell is stronger at night or after the air conditioner runs.
  • Look for flies or stains on a ceiling or a wall.
  • Compare the garage, the attic hatch and the closets.

Write down what you find and tell the dispatcher. The map you build in ten minutes can save an hour of searching.

What the timeline looks like.

A dead animal passes through a smell phase that depends on size and conditions. A mouse may cause a strong but shorter odor, and a rat produces something longer and heavier. No honest technician can promise a number of days. We can say that heat and humidity speed things up, and that the odor may outlast the animal if material soaks into insulation.

What a removal visit involves.

The technician locates the likely spot using the smell map, a flashlight and sometimes a camera on a flexible probe. Where access is easy, such as an attic or a crawl space, the animal is removed directly. Where it sits inside a finished wall, the plan may include a small opening that a drywall repairer can patch afterward.

Afterward, contaminated insulation may need to be removed, and the area is treated with a disinfectant before it is dried. Our odor control page covers the stages, and any repair is quoted on its own line.

The safety side.

Dead rodents and their waste can carry germs, so wear gloves and keep children and pets away. The CDC advises wetting the area with disinfectant, wiping with paper towels and bagging the waste, then washing your hands. Details are in the safety box below.

Prevent the next one.

The most common cause is bait. Poison works slowly, and a rodent that eats it often retreats into a wall or attic to die. Trapping, sealing and sanitation keep animals from reaching that stage. Sealing is covered on our exclusion page.

Living with the odor while you wait.

Run the air conditioner and keep the house closed during the day. Open windows only when the air outside is drier than the air inside, because humid air carries odor deeper into fabric and carpet. A shallow dish of baking soda near the strongest spot can take the edge off.

Wash fabrics that hold smell, such as curtains and throw pillows, once the source has been removed. Until then, the odor will keep returning. Patience is hard, though a proper removal is the quickest way to end it.

Before you call: keep the dust down.

The CDC describes one basic method for cleaning up droppings, and this box summarizes it.

  • The order is wet first and dry never.
  • Stale air concentrates whatever floats in it, so let the space breathe for about half an hour before you start.
  • Wear gloves throughout and wash up for a good while at the end.
  • Soak each dropping, and the surface beneath, in disinfectant or a bleach mix, then wait about five minutes before touching anything.
  • Knot the bag, throw it away and wash your hands again.
  • If you feel off later, tell a doctor about the cleanup and let them judge what matters.

Extra answers worth having.

For how long will the odor hang around?

Animal size, temperature and airflow all play a part. Heat and humidity quicken the process and carry the smell farther, and with no removal it can persist for a long stretch.

Will air fresheners help?

They mask the odor briefly. The source is still there, so the smell returns.

Can I cut open the wall myself?

We advise against it. You may hit wiring or plumbing, and opening a wall without precautions releases dust and odor.

Does the smell mean the poison worked?

It means an animal died somewhere. Poison can send a rodent into a hidden space first, which is why we avoid it near living areas.

Are flies a sign?

They can be. A sudden crop of small flies near a wall or vent sometimes accompanies a hidden carcass, though flies have other causes too.
