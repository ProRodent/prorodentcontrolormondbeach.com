The practical question is simpler: what does a lot with trees and a yard need checked? The sections below answer that for any property here.

Trees and the roof edge.

Live oaks and palms are common in this part of Florida, though we do not know which trees stand on your street. What matters is the gap between canopy and shingles. A branch that touches the roof, a frond lying in a valley or a limb resting on a wire can be a bridge.

  • Scan from the lawn for limbs, fronds and vines that brush the roof, gutter or siding.
  • Check that fascia, soffit panels and roof vents are intact.
  • Rake leaf litter back from the foundation.
  • Ask a tree service to cut a clear gap of several feet around the building.

If your lot backs onto a pond.

Some lots back onto a retention pond or a drainage edge. If yours does, mention it. Water edges often hold grass, reeds and cover, and rodents may travel along them. We make no claim about any pond near any particular home. The walk-through simply includes that border when you tell us it exists.

Weather and timing.

Heavy rain pushes burrowing animals toward higher ground. A hot afternoon sends them toward shade. A single gap can serve both needs in the same week. After any big storm, walk the building and photograph anything that changed.

Interim measures.

  • Keep children and dogs out of any room where droppings appear.
  • Pull on gloves before you touch anything stained or damp.
  • Skip glue boards, which also catch geckos and small birds.
  • Take pictures first and tidy later, because the mess shows the animal's travel route.

Before you call: a careful way to treat pellets and nests.

These lines paraphrase public health pages and stay general. For medical questions, a doctor is the right contact.

  • Brooms, shop vacuums and leaf blowers all spread dust, so none of them belongs near rodent waste.
  • Think of the first thirty minutes as ventilation time with no one inside.
  • Keep gloves on for the whole job, peel them off carefully and wash your hands afterward.
  • Soak each dropping, and the surface beneath, in disinfectant or a bleach mix, then wait about five minutes before touching anything.
  • One knotted bag can hold towels and a carcass together, with a second bag if the plastic is thin.
  • If you feel off later, tell a doctor about the cleanup and let them judge what matters.
