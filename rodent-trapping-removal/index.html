Where a trap belongs.

Night after night, rodents repeat the same routes. They follow walls, run along beams and use the same gap again and again. A trap set across that route intercepts them, while a trap in the middle of a room mostly gets ignored.

We read the lane from pellets, grease marks and dust trails. In a garage, that is usually the wall line behind stored bins. In an attic, it is a joist or a pipe. Outdoors, it can be a fence rail or the edge of a slab.

Choosing between snap traps and stations.

A snap trap is quick and shows results clearly, which helps us count animals. An enclosed station shields the mechanism, so it suits homes with dogs, cats or small children. The animal and the household decide which one we use.

  • Snap traps for confirmed lanes in attics, garages and behind appliances.
  • Enclosed stations for yards, patios and homes where pets roam.
  • Wall-hugging placement, because rodents prefer an edge to open floor.
  • Tamper-resistant locks wherever a child could reach.

The revisit schedule.

Traps are checked on dates you hear in advance. Each visit empties catches, resets or moves traps and records what changed. When the catches stop, sealing finishes the job and a final check confirms the quiet.

Trapping alone rarely lasts. Open entries keep inviting new animals, so the plan pairs removal with the exclusion work on the same timeline.

Safe handling.

Do not touch traps or captured animals with bare hands. If you find a trap sprung, leave it for the technician. Because droppings can carry germs, apply the CDC safety steps below to any cleanup you try yourself.

Before the technician comes.

  • Keep brooms and vacuums well away from droppings.
  • Leave the bleach in the cupboard until the scope of the job is clear.
  • Set a coin next to each gnaw mark when you photograph it to show scale.
  • Estimate how long ago the signs started.

Before you call, a plain cleanup routine.

Cleaning up before the technician comes? Follow the list top to bottom and stop at any step that feels uncertain.

  • Aged pellets disintegrate when brushed, which is why a light mist of disinfectant comes before any towel or hand.
  • After weeks of being shut, a garage, shed or attic holds stale air. Throw it open and stay out for half an hour.
  • Because hands move waste toward the mouth, gloves start the job and soap ends it.
  • Soak each dropping, and the surface beneath, in disinfectant or a bleach mix, then wait about five minutes before touching anything.
  • One knotted bag can hold towels and a carcass together, with a second bag if the plastic is thin.
  • Fever that will not break, or breathlessness, after contact with rodent waste is a reason to see a physician.

How trapping works: your questions.

Can traps be used where a dog or cat lives?

Sealed stations are our default, and snap traps go where animals cannot reach. List every pet for the technician so placement fits your home.

How many visits does trapping take?

The number of animals inside decides it. Revisit days are written into the plan beforehand, and we change them if the headcount turns out different.

What happens to the animals?

Captured rodents are handled and disposed of safely. We do not relocate them.

Should I buy my own traps?

It is better to hold off. Extra traps can scatter the animals and muddy the trail we are reading.

Do you use bait?

Trapping plus sealing is our preference. If a bait product is ever part of the plan, you learn which one and where it sits.
